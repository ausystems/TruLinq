The short answer

Search the company’s exact name in the official business registry of the state where it was formed, and confirm it is active or in good standing. Registration proves a business exists, not that it’s honest, so also check its licenses, address, phone, reviews and court records, and pay in a way you can dispute. Anyone who will only take gift cards, crypto or a wire from a stranger is a red flag.

In 2025, people told the Federal Trade Commission they lost about $16 billion to fraud, the most on record, and nearly $1 billion of it to scammers pretending to be businesses. There is no single database that says a company is honest. But there are a dozen free records that, taken together, make a convincing front very hard to keep up. Here they are, with what each one proves and, just as important, what it doesn’t.

The 12-point checklist

Work down the list. A real business passes almost all of it without effort; a fake one starts failing early.

  • It’s in the official business registry.Search the state where it was formed, and the state where it works with you.
  • Its status is active, or in good standing.Not dissolved, revoked, suspended or delinquent.
  • The names match.The legal name, any trade name, and the name on your contract, invoice or receipt.
  • It holds the licenses its work needs.Contractors, realtors, lenders and many trades are licensed, and the licence can be looked up.
  • The address is a real place it uses.A street address you can see, not only a mail drop or a registered agent’s office.
  • The phone number works, and you found it yourself.Not the number in the email, the ad or the caller ID.
  • Its website is older than its offer.Check when the domain was registered.
  • People who aren’t the business say it’s real.Independent reviews, complaints and its record with the BBB.
  • Its name, plus “scam” or “complaint”, turns up nothing alarming.The FTC’s own first step.
  • Its tax ID checks out.A signed Form W-9 from a vendor; the IRS list for a charity; SEC filings for a public company.
  • Court records and liens show no pattern of trouble.Worth doing before a large contract, a loan or a partnership.
  • It takes a payment you can dispute.Pressure to pay by wire, gift card, crypto or a payment app is the clearest sign of all.

Find it in the official registry

Every corporation, LLC and limited partnership in the United States is registered with a state, and the record is public and free to search. That record is the single most useful thing you can check, because a fake business either won’t be there or will be there under a different name.

Which registry to search

Start with the state where the business says it was formed, then the state where it works with you, since companies that operate in several states usually register in each one (called foreign qualification). In most states the registry belongs to the Secretary of State, but not in all: Arizona’s is run by its Corporation Commission, Virginia’s by its State Corporation Commission and Hawaii’s by the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs. The National Association of Secretaries of State keeps a page linking every state’s registry, which is the quickest way to the right one.

free to search in every state

What a record tells you

A typical entry shows the exact legal name, the type of entity, the date it was formed, its status, its registered agent and, depending on the state, its officers or managers, its annual filings and any trade names. Hawaii’s, for example, shows a status such as “Active” and a separate line saying whether the business is in good standing. Hawaii moved its search to a new portal in July 2026, Hawaii Business Express, and some older pages still link the retired one.

“Active” and “good standing” are not the same as honest

Good standing usually means the business has kept up with its state filings and fees. Colorado’s Secretary of State puts it plainly: good standing means “statutory filing requirements have been met with our office. However, this office does not review or verify information submitted in those filings.” The UK’s Companies House says the same of its register. Registration proves a company exists on paper; everything after this section is about whether it is what it claims to be.

Registered agents and trade names

The address on a registry record is often the registered agent’s, a person or firm that receives legal papers for the company, not the place the business operates. And a business may trade under a name that isn’t its legal one, called a DBA or trade name. Ask for the legal name behind any brand you are about to pay, and make sure that is the name on the contract.

Check its licenses

Many kinds of work need a license, and licenses are easier to check than most people think. Contractors, real estate agents, insurance agents, lenders, accountants, electricians and many health and beauty trades are licensed by a state board that publishes a lookup, and often a record of complaints and discipline. Hawaii, for instance, publishes a Business Check that shows both. Search “[your state] [trade] license lookup” and use the result on a .gov site. A license that doesn’t exist, has lapsed or belongs to a different name is one of the strongest signals you can get.

Check the address, the phone and the website

Real businesses leave a consistent trail. Fake ones reuse details, borrow them or make them up.

  • The address. Look it up on a map. Is it an office, a shop, a warehouse, a house? A mailbox store or a building with hundreds of companies at one suite number isn’t proof of fraud, but it means you should rely more on the other checks.
  • The phone. Call a number you found yourself, on the registry record, the license or the company’s long-standing website, not the one in the message. The FTC warns that scammers often fake their numbers, so don’t trust caller ID.
  • The website. Check when the domain was registered with ICANN Lookup, the official tool that replaced WHOIS for most domains in January 2025. A “20-year-old firm” on a domain registered last month deserves a hard look. Domain age is a clue, not proof: new businesses have new domains. Our guide to telling if a website is legit covers the rest.

Read what others say, carefully

Search the name with “scam” and “complaint”

The FTC’s own advice is to search online for the company’s name with words like “review,” “scam,” or “complaint” and see what other people say. A pattern of similar stories matters more than one angry review.

The Better Business Bureau

A BBB profile is worth reading for its complaint history and how the business answered. But know what it is: “BBB is not a government agency and holds no regulatory power,” and accreditation, which businesses pay for, is “voluntary and separate from the rating.” The BBB also says a rating is not a guarantee of how a business will perform. No profile at all is not a mark against a business.

Reviews can be bought

Since October 21, 2024, an FTC rule has banned fake reviews and testimonials, including ones written by AI, bought reviews, undisclosed reviews by insiders and fake “independent” review sites. Breaking it can cost up to $53,088 per violation. That makes fake reviews illegal, not rare. Trust reviews that are specific, spread over time and found on more than one site, and be wary of a wall of five-star praise posted in the same few weeks.

Complaints to the state

State attorneys general and consumer protection offices handle complaints against businesses and sometimes publish actions they have taken. Find yours through the National Association of Attorneys General or USA.gov.

For bigger deals: tax IDs, liens and lawsuits

Before a large contract, a loan or a partnership, go a level deeper.

Tax IDs

The IRS’s own number-matching service is only for businesses that file certain Forms 1099, and “EIN lookup” websites are not the IRS. The standard route is to ask a vendor for a signed Form W-9, which gives its legal name and taxpayer number, and to check that both match the registry. For a charity, the IRS’s Tax Exempt Organization Search confirms whether it can receive tax-deductible donations and shows its filings. For a public company, SEC EDGAR holds its filings for free.

Liens

UCC filings, kept by each state, let lenders record the assets a business has pledged as collateral. A search shows who it owes, and a sudden pile of new liens can be a sign of strain.

Lawsuits

Federal cases are searchable through PACER’s case locator, which charges $0.10 a page, capped at $3 a document, and waives the fees for anyone who spends $30 or less in a quarter. PACER covers federal courts only; state and county courts have their own portals. One lawsuit means little. Dozens from customers or suppliers with the same complaint mean a lot.

Pay in a way you can take back

How a business asks to be paid is one of the clearest signals you will get. The FTC is blunt: if the only way to pay is “by bank transfer, wire transfer, cryptocurrency, payment app, or gift card,” reports to the FTC show that’s a scam. Its advice is to pay by credit card whenever possible, because cards let you dispute a charge.

Two caveats. A credit card is not a shield: in 2025 it was the payment method people mentioned most often in fraud reports to the FTC, even though bank payments and cryptocurrency lost the most money. Its value is the right to dispute. And between businesses, bank transfers are normal, which is exactly why invoice fraud works: before you pay new bank details, call the business on a number you looked up yourself. Our guide to business email compromise explains why.

Checking a business in Canada or the UK

Canada

Corporations can be formed federally or in a province. Corporations Canada’s federal corporation search is the official source for federal corporations and shows their status, address and directors. For provincial companies, Canada’s Business Registries searches several registries at once: Alberta, British Columbia, Manitoba, Nova Scotia, Ontario, Québec, Saskatchewan and the federal one. For the other provinces and the territories, search their registries directly.

The United Kingdom

Every UK company is on Companies House, free to search, with its status, filing history, officers and people with significant control. Companies House says it does not check the accuracy of what is filed, but it now checks people: since 18 November 2025, directors and people with significant control must verify their identity, with existing directors given until their company’s next confirmation statement during a 12-month transition. Officer pages now show their identity verification status.

If you think you’ve already been scammed

  1. Contact your card issuer or bank today. Ask to dispute the charge or recall the transfer. Speed matters most with wires.
  2. Report it. In the US, to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and if it happened online, to the FBI at ic3.gov, which says to file “regardless of the amount lost.” In Canada, to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. In the UK, to Report Fraud, which replaced Action Fraud in December 2025.
  3. Tell your state’s attorney general or consumer protection office, which can act on patterns of complaints.
  4. Keep everything: messages, the website address, receipts and payment confirmations.
  5. Be wary of anyone offering to recover your money for a fee. Recovery scams follow the first scam.
